Building five agents live, on the mainstage

Domo put a live, unrehearsed build inside its CEO keynote. I wired five agents to governed enterprise data with Claude Code, on stage with Josh James, fixing and extending a running Domo instance in front of the company's largest audience of the year.

The setup

A pipeline that fails is easy to notice. One that keeps running while producing wrong numbers can go unnoticed for months, which makes it the more expensive problem to have.

The ask on stage went to the harder version: "look through every single ETL, find the ones that have issues, not just the ones that are broken." Misspellings in filters, empty columns, bad CASE statements, joins that look suspicious.

What it found

In a 2018–2020 HMDA mortgage dataflow, a purchaser_type CASE had eleven WHEN clauses that all tested the same value. Only the first branch could fire. Fannie Mae, Ginnie Mae, Freddie Mac: dead code, every row labelled "Not applicable."

The more interesting one was a single typo: Prinicial residence where the data said Principal residence. Every downstream filter looking for "Principal" returned zero rows, and nothing anywhere threw an error. Plus eight CASE statements with no ELSE, turning unmatched values into NULL.

The fix

Then the follow-up prompt, typed live: "how many of these can you fix for me right now, my job depends on it." Five dataflows repaired and saved in front of the room: purchaser codes corrected, the typo fixed, ELSE clauses added, join keys aligned, NULLIF guards put around three divisions. 25 fixes across 5 ETLs, zero errors.

Why it matters

None of that came down to model capability. It came down to governance, API access, and knowing which kinds of failure stay hidden.

How I work

How engagements start, and why the second one costs less.

Discovery first, then building inside their environment

Most of the useful work happens before anyone writes code. I spend the first sessions on the environment with their data and security people: the permissions model, where the data sits, and what stalled any previous attempt.

Teams in regulated industries have been burned by something that demoed well and then could not be deployed. That history tells me what they will agree to next. When a deployment has to clear a bank's security review, the work is everything around the model.

From there the build happens in their systems rather than a sandbox. I hit the connectors, authentication, pipelines and RBAC constraints early instead of at the end. I can connect an agent to a customer's live data in five minutes, sometimes ten; a usable prototype takes an afternoon, and production runs one to four weeks.

At that pace people react to working software. They get specific about what they want once they can see their own data moving, and the questions after that first session are the ones worth building around.

Each engagement makes the next one cheaper

Every engagement produces pattern libraries, implementation playbooks and readiness checklists. The next deployment inside that organization starts from those, and moves faster than the first one did.

The running cost compounds the same way: as the reusable components get tighter, token consumption falls and the model tier each task requires comes down with it.

Classification, extraction, routing and retrieval make up most of the volume. They run below frontier capability, which is why local models keep taking more of them. That makes engineering quality and inference spend the same lever, and most companies still budget them separately.

Enablement

The job is finished when the team doesn't need you.

Build the capability with the people closest to the problem. If a deployment only works while its builder is in the room, the team still depends on one person.

At Domo that meant building the practice, not only the deployments. Inside a year: a repeatable five-phase delivery model, an engineering team of twenty, a forty-seven-person Center of Excellence, and the build standards and hiring bar underneath them.

Those sixty-seven people now run the work; several hundred more have come through the training beneath them, across every department and inside customer organizations.

For the customer the deliverable is a team that can extend it: patterns they can copy, standards they hold each other to, and enough hands-on time that the next workload doesn't need a phone call.

Hackathons work better than documentation. Sitting with a customer's team in Japan beats shipping them a runbook. Capability spread that far outlasts the engagement; a single expert becomes the constraint.

Point of view

Two places the economics have already changed.

AI can do the task. The open question is how much of the operating model you rebuild once it can.

Full SaaS replacement

Most core SaaS functions a company pays for every month can now be rebuilt in-house, fitted to how that team works, and owned outright. The build-versus-buy line moved, and most companies have not caught up.

Agentic workflow optimization

The wins are in the workflows nobody documented: the handoffs, the approvals, the copy-paste between systems. Agents with human-in-the-loop checkpoints take the mechanical parts and give people back the judgement calls.
